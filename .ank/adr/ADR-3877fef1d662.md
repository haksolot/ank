## Context

A corpus written mostly by agents has no field that says so. `author` holds
whatever `$ANK_AGENT` resolved to — `seanl@sean-laptop` here, `claude-code@host-3`
in the examples — and nothing distinguishes the two shapes. The distinction the
whole authority model rests on, between what a person decided and what a model
produced, is not recorded anywhere except in the signed ratification commit, which
covers ADRs and only at the moment they are accepted.

The gap is largest exactly where it matters most. A `done_criteria` written by an
agent, claimed by an agent, and verified by a verifier an agent could edit is the
normal case in this repository, and `criteria_by: creator | claimer` is the only
signal about it — a signal that says which position wrote it, never what kind of
actor. A criterion no human ever read is indistinguishable from one a human wrote.

## What is taken from OKF, and what is not

The Open Knowledge Format v0.2 added trust signals for a closely related problem:
knowledge produced by agents, consumed by agents, with no way to tell how much
weight to put on any of it. Two of its choices are directly applicable.

The **actor convention** — `human:<id>`, `<producer>/<version>`, `process:<id>` —
costs one prefix and makes a machine-checkable distinction out of a string that
had none. Its trust tiers fall straight out: no reading is unverified, a reading
by a non-human actor is machine-confirmed, a reading by `human:` is
human-reviewed.

**`verified`** is the field ank lacks. `proof` anchors that something *ran*;
nothing anchors that somebody *read*. Those are different claims and a corpus
written by agents needs the second one more than a corpus written by people did.

And the reasoning behind both is one ank already follows: OKF records the signals
and refuses to compute a credibility score, on the grounds that a score is
subjective, does not port, and goes stale the moment it is written. That is
`check` emitting signals and never a verdict, arrived at independently.

## Rejected

**`generated: {by, at}`.** OKF pairs it with `verified`, and here it is redundant.
`author` records who produced the entity and `created` records when — and `created`
is hashed into the identifier, so it is anchored in a way a new field would not be.
Adding `generated` would give two fields answering one question, which is the
condition under which they eventually disagree.

**`stale_after`.** An absolute expiry date is a good idea for a data catalogue and
a bad one for a constraint. Section 11 already argues it: temporal decay is not
verifiable, a three-year-old constraint can be vital, and what ank detects instead
is structural death — a scope matching no file. A date would let a live constraint
be dismissed by the calendar.

**Requiring `verified` anywhere.** A required trust field is a field that gets
filled in to make the tool stop complaining, at which point it records nothing.
It is optional, and its absence is reported as a signal for agent-authored
entities only.

**Rewriting the 96 existing `author` values.** They were written before the
convention and mean what they meant. `check` reports the pre-convention set once
for the corpus and never per file, exactly as it already does for the 48 entities
that predate the field entirely — one line stays visible, one line per file
teaches a reader to stop reading `check`.

## On self-declaration, said plainly

ADR-9ede1ffd04e2 abolished the agent/human split in the CLI with the observation
that a wall whose bricks are self-declared identity is a sign and not a wall. This
ADR adds a sign, knowingly.

Nothing prevents an agent from writing `human:` in front of its own name, any more
than anything prevents it from setting `$ANK_AGENT` to a colleague's. What the
convention buys is that the honest case becomes legible and the dishonest one
becomes a lie somebody wrote down, which is the same trade the whole tool makes:
faking costs more than doing, and the record is what makes the difference
visible. Nothing in the refusal machinery consults these fields.
